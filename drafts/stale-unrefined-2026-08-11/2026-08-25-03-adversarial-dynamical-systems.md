*Field notes on what actually works — and on the limits nobody selling you a signal wants named.*

---

## The DM

The DM — the direct message, and it is almost always a direct message — shows up in some form every few weeks. A Discord ping, a comment reply, a YouTube pre-roll with a stock ticker scrolling behind a guy in a headset. The shape is always close to the same: *I trained a model on ten years of price data and it's up 340% this year — want the indicator?* Sometimes it's a neural net. Sometimes it's "AI." Sometimes it's dressed as a GPT wrapper that reads chart screenshots and tells you what to do next. The claim underneath is always the same claim: the market is a pattern, and given enough data and enough compute, a learning system will find the pattern and keep finding it.

I've spent the past year building a live-money trading system and the AI substrate around it — no revenue yet. I'd rather it prove itself before I decide what it becomes.

Which is a strange position to argue from, until you know how I got here. A friend got me interested in day trading in August of 2025. He pitched it the way everyone pitches it — learn this and you can replace your paycheck — the same rigamarole the course sellers run, and I noticed that at the time. I was interested anyway. At the same time, by happenstance, I was finding out that people were using AI to write real software. The two interests converged, and when I learned you could write a strategy in Pine Script, wire it through a broker bridge into a prop-firm account, and let it trade, a very large lightbulb went off.

So I am not writing this from outside the pitch. I started inside it. What changed between then and now is that I have spent enough time since reading the papers underneath my own tools to know that a Sharpe ratio on a slide — return divided by the volatility it took to earn it — is not evidence of anything until it survives the parts of the process designed to kill it.

Then a paper landed in my reading queue that asks a question nobody inside that pitch ever asks, and answers it with a proof instead of an equity curve. The question: for a given system, is there **any** learning algorithm that can succeed on it? Not *my* algorithm, not the DM's. Any algorithm, built any way, with any amount of data. That turns out to be a question with a real answer — and the answer has a floor in it that the DM's claim either stands on, or falls through.

Here's what the paper says, what it doesn't say, and what it changes about how I look at my own strategy portfolio.

## The claim under the claims

The paper is Colbrook, Mezić, and Stepanenko, "Adversarial dynamical systems characterize when data-driven learning succeeds or fails," published in *Nature Communications* on July 14, 2026 (DOI: 10.1038/s41467-026-74220-8). Cambridge and UCSB. Open access, so you can read it yourself rather than take my paraphrase as the last word — I'd rather you did.

The headline result: the authors construct *adversarial dynamical systems* — systems built, deliberately, in a way that respects the actual structure of the data you'd collect and still blocks any learning algorithm from succeeding. Not "succeeds badly." Blocks. For these constructed classes, success is capped at 50% regardless of how much data you have, how the algorithm is built, or whether it's deterministic or probabilistic. A coin flip does exactly as well as your best model, forever.

The part that should stop a trader mid-scroll is where these systems live. This isn't a pathological corner case cooked up to win an argument. The paper is explicit that adversarial dynamics "are not rare edge cases" — they arise in well-studied classes, including smooth dynamical systems on simple low-dimensional surfaces, doing a task as ordinary as learning a finite-dimensional linearized representation of the system through an autoencoder — a neural net that learns to squeeze a system's state down to a handful of numbers and rebuild it again, which is the standard way of asking a model to find the hidden structure underneath messy data. If you've ever heard someone describe a trading model as "just an autoencoder that learns the market's latent structure," this is the exact task class the paper shows can be unlearnable, structurally, not for lack of data or model capacity.

Two things keep this from being nihilism about machine learning, and both matter for anything I say later in this piece. First, the same paper hands you *provably optimal, convergent algorithms with certified error bounds* under broad conditions — a genuine advance in Koopman spectral analysis, the mathematics of representing nonlinear dynamics as linear spectral objects. The authors aren't saying learning is impossible. They're drawing the boundary between where it's guaranteed to work and where it's structurally blocked, and they hand you the tool for the side that works. Second, they validate the optimal-algorithm side on Arctic sea ice — CPU-trained, cheaper than the deep learning baselines, and it surfaces hidden decay modes with error bounds attached. This is a paper with teeth on both sides of the line it draws.

The line itself is the whole point. Most of what gets sold as "AI trading edge" never asks which side of that line the strategy sits on. This paper is the first place I've seen the question posed with enough precision to actually answer it.

## The soft case and the hard case

I want to draw a distinction here that the trading-education internet blurs constantly, because collapsing it is how people end up trusting backtests they shouldn't and dismissing frameworks that would have saved them.

**The soft case is backtest overfitting.** You ran a thousand parameter combinations, picked the best Sharpe, and reported it as if you'd found the true parameter rather than the luckiest one in your search space. This is *learnable-in-principle* territory — the underlying system isn't necessarily adversarial, your *estimate* is just contaminated by selection bias, temporal leakage, or a validation scheme that leaks information from the future into the past. The literature here is mature and specific: the Probability of Backtest Overfitting (Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado, and Zhu, 2014) gives you a formal number for how likely your "best" parameter set is just the median performer that got lucky. The Deflated Sharpe Ratio (Bailey and López de Prado, 2014) corrects your reported Sharpe for the number of trials you ran to find it — ten thousand tested combinations and a headline Sharpe of 2.0 might deflate to 0.8 once you account for how many shots you took. Combinatorial Purged Cross-Validation (López de Prado, 2018) prevents the specific leakage that plain train/test splits allow when your data is temporally autocorrelated, which financial time series always are. Run these three tools straight and you convert "my backtest looks good" into "my backtest looks good, and here's the number that tells you how much to discount that."

This is the discipline every serious quant shop already runs, in some form, whether or not they use these exact names for it. It is necessary. It is not, on its own, sufficient.

**The hard case is what this paper names.** Even a strategy that passes every soft-case check — clean CPCV folds, meaning the strategy held its edge across every purged train/test split and not just the flattering one; a defensible DSR, meaning the number survives being discounted for how many attempts it took to find; no PBO red flags, meaning your "best" parameters don't look like the ones that merely got lucky — can still be operating on a system where the underlying dynamics belong to an adversarial class. In that regime, no validation methodology rescues you, because the problem isn't that your estimate of the edge is noisy. The problem is that there is no algorithm, run on any amount of data, that can extract a better-than-coin-flip edge from that system in the first place. More data doesn't help. A cleverer cross-validation scheme doesn't help. The paper is explicit that even the two most obvious levers — collecting data over a longer window, or measuring more variables at higher fidelity — often can't be combined freely or chosen adaptively from the data itself; some problems are only solvable if those two kinds of data increase are applied in a specific order, not thrown at the problem together and hoped into working.

Here's the distinction stated as plainly as I can put it: PBO, DSR, and CPCV tell you whether you're fooling *yourself* about a signal that could, in principle, be found. The Colbrook-Mezić-Stepanenko result tells you that for some signals, there is no self to be fooled past — the signal isn't there to find, structurally, and passing every validation gate you own will not change that.

Most of the "AI has an edge in markets" content I see on Twitter and Discord doesn't even clear the soft-case bar — no PBO, no DSR, screenshots of one equity curve. But the loudest, most credentialed-sounding claims — the ones that cite real papers, run real cross-validation, and still promise a durable edge from a model trained purely on price history — are the ones that pretend the hard case doesn't exist. It's not laziness. It's that until three weeks ago, there wasn't a clean, citable, peer-reviewed way to say "some of what you're calling learnable might not be," short of a hand-wave about efficient markets.

## What this looks like in a real strategy portfolio

I run a small book of live and paper strategies on prop-firm futures accounts — MNQ, MGC, SIL — through a system I've been building called Trident Forge. Two of the strategies in that book sit at opposite ends of the soft-case/hard-case spectrum, and the contrast is the clearest way I know to make this concrete.

**The market maker.** One of my live strategies is a market-making implementation built directly from Avellaneda and Stoikov's 2008 paper, "High-Frequency Trading in a Limit Order Book" (*Quantitative Finance*, 8(3):217–224). The model doesn't predict where price is going. It manages inventory risk — quoting a reservation price around the current mid, adjusted for how much inventory the strategy is currently carrying, with a spread that widens as realized volatility or time-to-flat pressure increases. It captures the spread it provides, not a directional call on the next candle. That framing matters for where it sits relative to this paper: a strategy that isn't claiming to forecast the trajectory of a dynamical system is, definitionally, exposed to less hard-case risk than one that is. Its risk is almost entirely soft-case — did I calibrate the arrival-intensity and inventory-aversion parameters to noise in a specific backtest window, or to something that holds up out of sample.

And the soft case bit me on exactly this strategy, which is the part of the story worth telling straight. An earlier, heuristic version of this market maker had backtested well — a profit factor that looked strong across more than a thousand simulated trades. When I audited it against the source paper line by line, the number turned out to be a fill-model artifact: the backtest's assumptions about how resting orders got filled were flattering the strategy in a way live fills never would. I pulled it from live capital, rebuilt it faithfully against the paper's actual equations for reservation price and spread, and only let the rebuild back near live money after it ran in parallel with the retired version long enough to show the two agreed. That's the soft case working as designed — not a strategy that failed because the underlying dynamics were adversarial, but one that failed because my validation wasn't strict enough on the first pass, and the fix was more rigor, not less ambition.

**The pattern strategy.** Elsewhere in the same portfolio sits a short-only strategy built around a candlestick continuation pattern — a specific, intrinsically rare multi-candle formation with volume and momentum confirmation layered on. It sits in ghost trading for verification — generating signals against live data without ever touching an order — specifically because a strategy like this is a directional bet dressed in pattern-recognition language, and that is exactly the shape of claim the hard case should make you suspicious of. A recurring visual pattern in historical candles *looks* like structure. Whether it's structure the market will keep producing, or a mechanically-generated regularity that happened to correlate with profitable moves in the sample I have — the kind of thing Stefan Nagel's 2025 critique of a well-known "virtue of complexity" paper showed can hide inside an apparently sophisticated model, once you go looking for the mechanical artifact underneath the impressive-looking result — is not a question CPCV alone can answer. CPCV tells you the pattern held up across purged folds. It does not tell you whether the pattern belongs to a class of dynamics where no amount of folding would ever reveal that the apparent edge is really structure, versus noise that happens to rhyme with structure for as long as you've been watching.

I don't have a clean way to prove that strategy isn't operating in an adversarial regime. Nobody does, for any pattern-based strategy, because the paper's whole point is that this kind of proof doesn't exist in general — you can show a system *is* learnable under the right conditions, but you cannot, from finite data alone, prove a given system *isn't* adversarial. So the honest response isn't a cleverer backtest. It's the posture the strategy is already in: extended live-shadow observation before a single dollar is at risk, a strict promotion bar, and — this is the part that matters — a standing willingness to pull a strategy back to paper the moment its live behavior diverges from its validated behavior, the same demotion path that's already moved other strategies in this book from live capital back to shadow status when their edge stopped confirming in real time. The gate isn't "did it pass CPCV once." The gate is "does it keep passing, indefinitely, with the trapdoor always open."

That's the shape of the spectrum. A theory-grounded strategy with an explicit, non-directional objective sits closer to the learnable side and carries mostly soft-case risk. A pattern-recognition strategy making an implicit directional claim sits closer to the region this paper says might be unlearnable no matter how it's validated, and the only honest response is permanent vigilance, not a better backtest.

## The LLM connection

The paper doesn't stop at Koopman operators and sea ice. Buried in the introduction is a paragraph that connects directly to something I'd already been thinking about from a different angle, and it's worth walking through slowly because it changes how I read every "let the LLM pick your trades" pitch I run into.

The authors note that large language models generate output — sentences, over a state space of words — through one-step-ahead prediction, a process that is itself linked to Koopman operators. The adversarial systems they construct tend to have Koopman operators with a *continuous* spread of frequencies, which is the mathematical signature of chaotic dynamics. A continuous spectrum gives you short-term accuracy — the next token, the next word, is highly predictable given the recent context — and simultaneously guarantees long-term unpredictability, because chaotic systems are sensitive to initial conditions by definition. Small changes in the starting point compound into large divergences downstream. The authors state plainly that this mirrors how small prompt changes in an LLM can cause wildly divergent outputs, and that their result may help explain a limitation baked into autoregressive architectures themselves, not one that better fine-tuning quietly removes.

I read that paragraph next to an essay I'd bookmarked from a few days earlier — "The Loop and the Cut," which works through a biological theory of meaning built on Howard Pattee's epistemic cut: the idea that interpretation isn't a state a system holds, it's a rotating loop of write, read, and rewrite, where the system's own history of interpreting keeps remaking the boundary between its instructions and its actions. The essay draws a distinction that's easy to skip past and hard to unsee once you've seen it: an LLM's *training* rewrite is exogenous — a human sets the loss function, curates the data, defines the reward model, decides when to stop. That's closer to a breeder selecting cattle across generations than to a cell rewriting its own chromatin in response to what it just did. And once inference starts, the weights are frozen. There is no rotating cut where the system's own reading of its output rewrites the marks that generated that reading. When a model apologizes, or claims a self, or works through a paradox under pressure, that's pattern completion over the *topic* of self-reading — not the system taking ownership of its own interpretive process the way a living loop does.

Put the two together and you get a sharper diagnosis than either piece offers alone. The Nature paper gives you the spectral mechanism — why autoregressive drift under small perturbations is a property of the architecture, not a training gap. The essay gives you the governance question that mechanism implies — because if the drift is architectural and the only rewrite loop available is exogenous and human-authored, then no amount of retraining closes the gap from the inside. Nobody is going to RLHF their way to a model that reads its own trading output and revises the criterion for what counts as a good trade, because the criterion was never the model's to revise in the first place. It belongs to whoever wrote the loss function.

So here's the question I've started asking, out loud, whenever someone shows me an LLM "picking trades": who authors the rewrite criterion? If the answer is "the model learns as it goes," push on that — because inference-time weights don't move, and the illusion of learning-as-it-goes is usually a longer context window doing pattern completion, not a rewrite loop closing on itself. If the answer is "we retrain nightly on new data," that's a real answer, but it's exogenous rewrite on a slow clock, run by a person, subject to every soft-case and hard-case question already on the table for any other model. Neither answer gets you out from under the ceiling this paper draws. It just tells you which side of the ceiling you're standing on.

## The reframe

None of this is a case for "AI can't trade." That claim is wrong on its face — the same paper that draws the 50% ceiling for adversarial classes also hands you provably convergent algorithms for the classes that aren't adversarial, and validates them beating deep learning baselines on a hard forecasting problem in its own right. The ceiling is a ceiling on a specific, well-defined family of learning tasks under adversarially-constructed dynamics. It is not a verdict on machine learning, and it is not a verdict on trading.

What it is, is a forcing function for specificity. The honest question was never "does AI have an edge in markets." The honest question is: *which part of your strategy sits inside the learnable regime, and which part is a bet you're making that it does?* A market-making model that manages inventory against realized volatility, anchored to a paper with a real proof behind it, is making a narrower and more defensible claim than a model that ingests years of OHLC bars and outputs a directional signal with no theoretical anchor beyond "the pattern held in backtest." Both can be built with AI. Both can look impressive in a demo. Only one of them can point to a reason, beyond the backtest itself, for why the thing it's claiming to have learned should generalize.

That specificity is the entire difference between a practitioner and a copywriter selling an indicator. The copywriter's pitch works precisely because it never has to say which regime the claim lives in — "AI found the pattern" is unfalsifiable marketing exactly because it refuses to specify anything a paper like this could test. A practitioner should be able to say, for any strategy in the book: here is the theoretical anchor, here is the soft-case validation it passed, here is the hard-case exposure I can't fully rule out, and here is the discipline that manages the part I can't rule out. If a strategy can't produce those four things on request, it doesn't matter how the signal was generated. It's a story, not a system.

## What a serious builder actually does

Concretely, here's what changes and what doesn't, once you take the hard case as real rather than academic.

The soft-case discipline doesn't go anywhere — it gets more load-bearing, not less. Every new strategy gets a Deflated Sharpe Ratio before its backtest number is trusted, a Probability of Backtest Overfitting score before it's compared against anything else in the book, and Combinatorial Purged Cross-Validation instead of a plain train/test split, because financial time series leak information across a naive split in ways that make the reported number a fiction. None of that changes.

What changes is the posture toward the strategies that pass all three anyway. Passing CPCV, PBO, and DSR tells you the strategy isn't fooling you about noise it found in your particular sample. It does not tell you the strategy isn't operating in an adversarial regime, because that's a property of the underlying dynamics, not of your validation methodology — and this paper is explicit that no single-sequence limiting procedure, run adaptively on the data you already have, can certify you out of that uncertainty. So the response has to be structural, not statistical: hold pattern-based, theory-thin strategies to an extended live-shadow period regardless of how clean their backtest reads, and keep a real demotion path — live capital back to paper, permanently open, no ego about it — for any strategy whose live behavior starts to diverge from what validation promised. A strategy earning that trust back has to re-earn it in parallel with its own history, not on the strength of a single retrospective number.

The other piece worth adopting directly from the paper's own method is adversarial construction as an analytical tool, not just a mathematical curiosity. The authors didn't find these unlearnable systems by accident — they built them, deliberately, to see exactly where learning breaks. That's the same move I try to run before I trust any validation pass on my own work: don't just check whether the strategy survives the data I have, construct the specific scenario engineered to break the claim — the regime shift, the fill-model assumption flipped against you, the parameter perturbed exactly where the strategy is most confident — and see if it survives that. A validation pass that never had to survive an adversary constructed to kill it hasn't told you very much.

And the multiple-limits point is worth sitting with, because it's the one most people skip past. More historical data and richer measurement both feel like unambiguous wins, and the instinct is to grab both and see what improves. This paper's math says that instinct can be exactly backwards — some representations are only reachable through a specific ordering of those two limits, not through throwing both at the problem simultaneously and picking whichever combination scores best on the sample you already have. That last move — pick the best-scoring combination after the fact — is precisely the multiple-comparisons trap that DSR exists to catch. The paper gives a deeper, structural reason to distrust it that goes beyond "you tested too many things": for some problems, the order itself is load-bearing, not just the count.

No amount of retraining fixes a strategy sitting inside the adversarial class. That sentence is the whole piece, compressed. The response isn't a bigger model or a longer backtest. It's knowing which regime you're in, building the discipline that manages the regime you can't fully verify, and never letting a clean validation pass talk you out of the vigilance the paper says you still need.

## Closing

I'll leave the two companion pieces for anyone who wants to go further than I've taken it here: the Colbrook, Mezić, and Stepanenko paper itself (DOI: 10.1038/s41467-026-74220-8, open access, worth the read even if the Koopman-operator math takes a second pass), and Colbrook's earlier survey, "The Multiverse of Dynamic Mode Decomposition Algorithms," for the broader landscape this result sits inside.

I don't think the takeaway here is caution for its own sake. I think it's the opposite — it's permission to stop pretending every claim needs to sound the same amount of confident. Some of what I've built has a real anchor and a narrower claim, and I trust it more for it. Some of it is still in the shadows, earning the right to be trusted, and it should stay there until it's earned it — not because I lack conviction, but because the paper gave me a real reason to hold the line.
